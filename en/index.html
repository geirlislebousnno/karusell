Seen from the merry-go-round, the path curves, because the platform turns underneath the ball while it travels. This is the Coriolis effect.

When the ball is released it carries both the inward throw speed and the sideways speed of the rim (ωR). Over the ground it therefore travels in a straight, slanted line. Seen from the merry-go-round, it looks as if a sideways force, the Coriolis force, bends the path. It acts towards the thrower’s right when the platform turns anticlockwise, as in the Northern Hemisphere.

At the centre the merry-go-round has no speed, so the ball moves straight outwards over the ground. While the ball travels, the catcher moves with the rim. Seen from the merry-go-round, the path bends into a spiral, because the Coriolis force acts towards the thrower’s right when the platform turns anticlockwise. To hit, the thrower must aim ωR/v radians ahead of the catcher.

What is the Coriolis effect?

The Coriolis effect is the apparent deflection of a moving object when it is observed from a rotating reference frame, such as a merry-go-round or the Earth. No real force pushes the ball sideways. The deflection appears because the observer is rotating.

To describe the motion from the rotating frame, we introduce a fictitious force, the Coriolis force:

F = −2m ω × v

When the velocity is perpendicular to the rotation axis, its magnitude is 2mωv. The force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction of motion but not the speed.

Why does the path curve?

When the ball is released it has the rim’s sideways speed ωR in addition to the throw speed. Over the ground it then moves in a straight line. The thrower follows the platform around a circle and keeps turning to the left when it rotates anticlockwise. From the thrower’s point of view, the ball therefore drifts to the right.

When the ball is thrown from the centre outwards, it gets no sideways speed, because the platform is at rest at the centre. Over the ground the ball moves straight out along a radius while the catcher on the rim moves on. Seen from the merry-go-round, the path becomes a spiral. Here the deflection is a pure Coriolis effect, with no contribution from the rim speed.

The radius of curvature in the rotating frame is roughly v/(2ω), so a slow throw or fast rotation gives a tighter curve. On the merry-go-round the centrifugal force mω²r also acts outwards. It makes the trace drift outwards, which is why it forms a loop rather than a clean circle.

The Coriolis effect on Earth: wind, low pressure and ocean currents

Seen from above the North Pole, the Earth rotates anticlockwise, just like the merry-go-round with positive ω. That is why winds, ocean currents and projectiles are deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.

Air and water moving freely travel in clockwise circles in the Northern Hemisphere. These are called inertial oscillations, and they turn the same way as the ball in the simulation. Low-pressure systems still rotate anticlockwise. The pressure force pulls air towards the centre, the Coriolis force bends it to the right, and the air ends up circling the low with the centre on its left. Around a high, air circulates clockwise.

The Earth’s angular velocity is only 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s. The effect matters over large distances and long times, but it does not decide which way water swirls in a sink.

Try it yourself

  • Adjust the aim angle so the ball hits the centre at ω = 0.6 rad/s and v = 4 m/s. Calculate the angle first, then check it in the simulation. Hint: the sideways component of the throw must cancel the rim speed ωR.
  • Choose “From the centre outwards”. How many degrees ahead of the catcher must you aim to hit at ω = 0.6 rad/s and v = 4 m/s? Hint: the ball takes t = R/v to reach the rim. How far has the catcher turned in that time?
  • Lower the throw speed to 1–2 m/s. Why does the trace form a loop when thrown from the edge, but not from the centre?
  • Set ω to a negative value. The path now curves to the left, as in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Set ω = 0. The path is straight in both views.
  • Double ω and watch how the Coriolis acceleration and the curvature change.
